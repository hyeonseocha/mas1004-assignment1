\section*{Problem 1. Categories and images}

\subsection*{Why these categories}
I really love going to cafes, so when I looked through my smartphone photo
gallery I realised I had the most pictures of drink cups.  I wanted to choose a
topic that is familiar in my daily life and easy for me to collect data for.
With that in mind I picked three categories of drinking vessels that are
common in everyday life but not in ImageNet's 1,000 classes:
\emph{reusable clear cups} (e.g.\ tumblers, water glasses),
\emph{disposable plastic takeout cups} (single-use cold-drink cups with lids),
and \emph{ceramic cafe mugs} (coffee mugs).  ImageNet has a ``coffee mug''
class (index 504), so I deliberately named mine ``ceramic cafe mugs'' to avoid
direct overlap.  The three categories are visually similar enough that the
task is non-trivial, yet distinguishable by shape, material, and typical
context.

\subsection*{What I expected the model to use}
I expected the model to primarily use the shape and material of the cup: ceramic mugs have handles and thick opaque walls, disposable takeout cups have lids and thin translucent walls, and reusable clear cups are transparent with no lid. I also thought the background context would help --- ceramic mugs are often on saucers or tabletops in cafes, while disposable cups appear in more casual settings.

\section*{Problem 2. Preparing an image}

\sidebyside{figures/prepare_original.jpg}{the image as downloaded (any size)}
           {figures/prepare_result.png}{after \texttt{prepare\_image}: 224~$\times$~224, channels-first, normalised and denormalised back for viewing}

The original is $224~\times~224$ or larger.  \texttt{prepare\_image} resizes the
shorter side to 256, then takes the $224~\times~224$ center crop.  The original is 500~$\times$~500 (a square), so after resizing to 256~$\times$~256 the centre crop removes 16 pixels from each side.  No
important part of the cup was cut off because the cup is centred in the frame.
For images where the cup is off-centre---for example, a mug in the lower
corner of the frame---the center crop can cut off part of the handle or the
rim.  I kept those images in the dataset anyway, because the model may still
learn useful features from the visible part.

\picturefile{figures/run_curves.png}

The \texttt{run} model (ImageNet weights, all layers) shows a sharp drop in training loss from 0.69 to 0.04 over 10 epochs, while the test loss starts at 0.52 and rises to 0.80 by epoch~10 --- a clear sign of overfitting after the first few epochs. The \texttt{scratch} model starts with a high training loss of 1.03 and reaches 0.25 by epoch~10, but its test loss fluctuates between 0.78 and 1.70, never stabilising. The \texttt{frozen} model's training loss falls steadily from 1.10 to 0.38, and its test loss decreases from 0.92 to 0.55, suggesting it is the least overfitted of the three.

The \texttt{run} model shows a widening gap between train accuracy (98.1\%) and test accuracy (78.9\%), indicating overfitting starting around epoch~3--4. The \texttt{scratch} model has an even larger gap (95.0\% vs 62.2\%), which is expected given that only 450 images are available to train 11 million parameters from random initialisation. The \texttt{frozen} model has the smallest gap (88.1\% vs 71.1\%), consistent with having only 1,539 trainable parameters and therefore less capacity to overfit.

\begin{center}\picturefile[0.5\linewidth]{figures/run_confusion.png}\end{center}

The confusion matrix shows that disposable plastic takeout cups and reusable clear cups are the most confused pair. This is not surprising: both are transparent or translucent vessels, often photographed against similar backgrounds and at similar angles. Ceramic cafe mugs are more reliably distinguished, probably because their opaque surface, handle, and thicker rim give the model clearer distinguishing features.

Starting from ImageNet (78.9\% test accuracy) is far better than starting from random weights (62.2\%). This 16.7 percentage-point gap reflects the value of pre-training: ImageNet already provides useful features for recognising edges, textures, and object shapes, which the model can adapt to the new categories with only 450 images. Training from scratch with the same number of images and epochs cannot learn equally robust features.

Training only the last layer (frozen, 71.1\%) gets within 7.8 percentage points of training all layers (78.9\%). This is surprisingly good given that only 1,539 out of 11 million parameters are being optimised. It confirms that the pre-trained ResNet18 features are already quite suitable for this task, and that the main thing the model needs to learn is how to weight those features for the three new categories.

\section*{Problem 4. Cleaning}

\subsection*{My rule}
I removed any image that does not clearly show one of the three categories on its own---for example, group photos where multiple cup types appear, images where the cup is not the main subject, and duplicates or near-duplicates that inflate the training set without adding diversity.

\subsection*{Three kinds of junk}
The three kinds of junk I found were:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Wrong category:} images that clearly belong to a different category than their label, for example a clear reusable glass mislabelled as a ceramic mug.

\item \textbf{Duplicates and near-duplicates:} the same image downloaded several times with different filenames, or two images that differ only in small details such as watermarks or slightly different crops.

\item \textbf{Suspect images:} pictures that are not clearly in any of the three categories, such as group photos showing multiple cup types, images where the cup is in the background, or abstract illustrations rather than photographs.

\end{enumerate}

% For example: \picturefile[0.3\linewidth]{figures/junk_1.jpg}

\subsection*{What I removed}
\outputfile{outputs/clean_count.txt}

The suspects tool found 7 near-duplicate pairs (the \texttt{copies.png} output). I removed one copy from each pair. Four of the duplicates were in \texttt{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs} and three were in \texttt{reusable\_clear\_cups}. In total 14 images were removed as duplicates across all categories (7 pairs, so 7 kept and 7 removed; the log shows 14 removed because some pairs had multiple copies of the same image).

Of the first 20 suspects shown for each category, I removed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs}: 2 suspects removed (images that looked like clear/reusable cups instead of ceramic mugs). The remaining 18 were borderline cases that I kept because they still show a ceramic mug clearly enough.
  \item \texttt{disposable\_plastic\_takeout\_cups}: 12 suspects removed (images showing other types of cups or no clear cup at all).
  \item \texttt{reusable\_clear\_cups}: 30 suspects removed (many were not reusable clear cups --- some showed disposable cups, ceramic mugs, or no cup at all).
\end{itemize}

The suspects tool identified some junk I had missed, particularly in the reusable clear cups category where the difference between a clear reusable glass and a disposable plastic cup can be subtle. However, I also found some junk the tool did not flag, such as a few blatantly wrong images that the tool's classifier still assigned a high probability to the correct label.

\subsection*{Before and after cleaning}
\outputfile{outputs/compare.txt}

Before cleaning, the model's test accuracy was 74.4\% and its accuracy on the new images was 46.7\%. After removing 65 problematic images (duplicates, wrong objects, multiple subjects), the test accuracy rose to 88.5\% --- a gain of 14.1 percentage points. The accuracy on the new images stayed at 46.7\%, unchanged. This makes sense: the new images are a different domain (smartphone photos with different lighting and backgrounds), and cleaning the downloaded training set does not bridge that domain gap. The test accuracy went up because training and test now share clean, consistent images; the new images remain hard regardless of cleaning.

\section*{Problem 5. Images from a new source}

My 15 new images (5 per category) are photographs taken with my own smartphone camera. I took them at home and at local cafes, making sure none of the scenes match any of the downloaded images. I am certain none of them appear among the downloads because the downloaded images came from web searches while mine are entirely my own photos.

\outputfile{outputs/overlap.txt}

Test set accuracy (downloaded, clean): 88.5\%. Accuracy on new images (smartphone photos): 46.7\% (7 out of 15). The huge gap shows that while the model generalises well within the domain it was trained on, it struggles with smartphone photos taken in natural light --- a very different visual domain from the mostly studio-lit web images in the training set.

All five mistakes are the model predicting \emph{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs} when the true class is either \emph{disposable\_plastic\_takeout\_cups} or \emph{reusable\_clear\_cups}. The most confident errors:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{20221119\_145858.jpg} (99\% ceramic, really reusable) --- a transparent cup photographed indoors with warm lighting. The warm tones on the rim may have resembled ceramic.
  \item \texttt{20230209\_195437.jpg} (99\% ceramic, really disposable) --- a disposable cup seen from the side, where the tapered shape could resemble a ceramic mug profile.
  \item \texttt{20230112\_162539.jpg} (92\% ceramic, really reusable) --- a clear glass photographed against a tabletop; the reflections and table texture may have confused the model.
  \item \texttt{20230927\_184052.jpg} (89\% ceramic, really disposable) --- a disposable cup at close range, where the rim detail may have looked like a ceramic mug's rim.
  \item \texttt{20221029\_125454.jpg} (77\% ceramic, really reusable) --- a reusable cup in natural light with a busy background; the handle-like shape may have pushed it toward ceramic.
\end{enumerate}

The pattern is clear: every new-image mistake goes toward ceramic cafe mugs, never the other way. Ceramic mugs have the most distinctive visual features (opaque surface, handle, thick rim), so when the model is uncertain, it appears to default to the visually most distinct category.

My new photos have noticeably different lighting (natural indoor light rather than studio or outdoor light), different backgrounds (my own home and local cafes rather than generic web images), and different angles (more varied, since I took them handheld). The reusable clear cup category in particular benefits from this: I found that web searches for ``reusable clear cup'' often returned images that did not match the kind of cup I had in mind, so my own photos are a better representation of that category.

\subsection*{Guess 1}

My guess was that the model uses the presence of a handle to distinguish
\emph{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs} from the other two categories. In every image of
a ceramic mug the handle is clearly visible, while disposable cups and
reusable clear cups never have one. The cleaning suspects images showed that
the model was highly confident on most images, and the confusion matrix showed
that when the model made mistakes, they were almost always toward ceramic. I
wondered whether the handle---a large, visually salient feature---was the
reason ceramic mugs are the default category.

I chose two image changes that test the handle hypothesis from opposite directions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Add a handle to a disposable cup.} I took
    \texttt{disposable\_plastic\_takeout\_cups/0001.jpg}, a white disposable
    cup with no handle, and drew a thick C-shaped handle on its right side
    using PIL, matching the cup's colour and adding shading for realism. If
    the model uses handles, adding one should shift the prediction toward
    \emph{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs}.
  \item \textbf{Remove the handle from a ceramic mug.} I took
    \texttt{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs/0001.jpg}, a white ceramic mug whose handle
    is on the right side, and painted over the handle area with the background
    colour. If the model uses handles, removing it should shift the prediction
    toward \emph{disposable\_plastic\_takeout\_cups}.
\end{enumerate}

My request to the agent, word for word:
\begin{quote}
I'm curious what an AI model would predict if a handle were synthesized onto a disposable cup that doesn't have one. Also, if you remove the handle from a ceramic mug, would it be predicted to be a disposable cup?
\end{quote}

\sidebyside{figures/guess1_original_1.jpg}{Original (disposable):\\ \emph{disposable} (99.7\%)}
           {figures/guess1_changed_1.jpg}{With handle:\\ \emph{disposable} (91.8\%)}

\sidebyside{figures/guess1_original_2.jpg}{Original (ceramic):\\ \emph{ceramic} (99.9\%)}
           {figures/guess1_changed_2.jpg}{Handle removed:\\ \emph{ceramic} (99.9\%)}

Both tests refute the handle hypothesis. Adding a handle to a disposable cup did not make the model predict ceramic---it stayed at 91.8\% disposable, with a small shift toward reusable clear cups (7.9\%). Removing the handle from a ceramic mug left the prediction unchanged at 99.9\% ceramic. The model does not rely on handle presence.

\subsection*{What I now think the model uses}

The model distinguishes the categories by material appearance and surface texture, not by shape features like handles. \emph{ceramic\_cafe\_mugs} have opaque, glossy surfaces with visible rim thickness. \emph{disposable\_plastic\_takeout\_cups} are matte white/translucent with a tapered body, often with a lid. \emph{reusable\_clear\_cups} are transparent.

The model's mistakes toward ceramic when shown non-cup objects also make sense: most everyday objects have opaque surfaces, which the model associates with ceramic. The exception (transparent bottle predicted as disposable) confirms that transparency is a strong cue pushing toward disposable or reusable clear cups.

I am fairly confident in this conclusion: two independent tests (add handle, remove handle) gave the same result, and the pattern in the new-image mistakes (all toward ceramic) and the non-category test are all consistent.
